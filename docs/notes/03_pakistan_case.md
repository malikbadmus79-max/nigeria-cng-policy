# Reading 3: Pakistan, the CNG boom and collapse [S23–S26]

## Summary

1. Pakistan introduced CNG in 1992. Cheap, regulated gas prices drove rapid adoption, and by 2011 it was the world's largest CNG user, with 2.5m vehicles, 3,329 stations and about 21% of vehicles converted (S23).
2. Demand outgrew domestic gas production. From 2008 the government stopped issuing new station licences (S24).
3. In shortages, gas was diverted to households and power. CNG stations were shut for weeks each winter, e.g. all stations in Sindh and Balochistan from 1 Dec 2021 to 15 Feb 2022 (S25, S26).
4. CNG use fell 83%, from 325 MMCFD in FY2011–12 to 55 MMCFD in FY2024–25, while petrol and diesel use kept rising (S24).
5. CNG went from a mainstream fuel to a niche, and its future now depends on imported LNG at much higher prices.

## Three lessons for Nigeria

1. **Adoption can outrun gas supply.** Pakistan's cheap CNG pulled in millions of vehicles faster than its gas fields could serve them. Nigeria has large reserves (210+ tcf, S02), but its processing and delivery bottlenecks (Ajaokuta loading delays, port clearing) are the binding limit, not reserves.
2. **When gas is scarce, transport loses.** Pakistan's allocation rules put homes and power first, which stranded CNG drivers. Nigeria needs a clear rule on where transport sits in the gas supply priority order before shortages hit.
3. **Policy reversals strand investment.** The licence ban, supply cuts and price swings destroyed trust among station owners and drivers. Nigeria's overnight price jump (₦230 to ₦380/scm in Sept 2025) and incentives issued by executive order carry the same risk.

Some figures in the Arab News articles (national station counts) look mis-stated, so those articles are cited only for the supply-cut mechanism. The OGRA report itself has not yet been reviewed.
